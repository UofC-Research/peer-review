# Publish or Perish: Quantifying the Impact of Peer Review on Scientific Work

## Background

“Publish or perish” is a pervasive norm across scientific disciplines, making peer review an unavoidable stage in the research lifecycle. However, conventions of peer review vary widely across fields: in some cases, reviewers request substantial changes to study design, analysis, or interpretation, while in others feedback is limited to minor clarifications or presentation.

Although reviewer suggestions may be constructive, they are not always perceived as aligned with authors’ original scientific motivations. When extensive or disruptive revisions are requested, authors’ willingness and capacity to implement changes may depend on contextual pressures such as career stage, funding environment, disciplinary norms, and the competitiveness of the publication system in which they operate. In some fields—particularly in biomedical research—peer review may involve substantial post-hoc modification of analyses or framing, raising questions about whether such changes consistently enhance statistical rigor or instead constrain academic autonomy.

Despite the central role of peer review, empirical evidence quantifying **how manuscripts change during the review process**, and how those changes vary across contexts, remains limited.

---

## Aims

Peer review is widely treated as a central quality-control mechanism in scientific publishing. Despite its normative importance, there is limited empirical evidence quantifying **how much manuscripts actually change as they move from preprint to peer-reviewed publication**, and whether those changes vary systematically across scientific, institutional, and career contexts.

The primary aim of this project is to **quantify observable changes in research manuscripts associated with the peer-review process** and to **examine how the magnitude and nature of these changes are associated with contextual factors** surrounding authors and research environments. The study does not assume that peer review necessarily improves scientific work, nor that observed changes are inherently beneficial. Instead, it seeks to document *what changes*, *in which dimensions*, and *under what conditions*.

Specifically, this project examines associations between quantified manuscript changes and the following contextual factors:

- **Funding mechanisms of authors’ institutions**, distinguishing between systems in which faculty salaries are largely supported by external grants (“soft-funded”) versus those primarily supported by institutional or state funding (“hard-funded”)

- **Academic system context and competitive structure**, including broad regional systems (e.g., European, North American, and Asian academic environments) that differ in evaluation norms and career incentives

- **Career stage of key authors**, including first authors and corresponding authors at different stages (research track, tenure track, tenured), who may engage with peer-review feedback differently

- **Scientific discipline**, including (but not limited to) mathematics, statistics, computer science, physics, chemistry, biology, and medicine, which vary substantially in publication norms and review practices

To support these aims, the project quantifies manuscript-level changes by comparing **preprint versions** hosted on **:contentReference[oaicite:0]{index=0}**, **:contentReference[oaicite:1]{index=1}**, and **:contentReference[oaicite:2]{index=2}** with their corresponding peer-reviewed journal publications. Changes are measured using preregistered indicators of statistical rigor and reporting transparency, evaluated symmetrically across versions.

The analytic goals of the project are twofold:

1. **Quantification of change**  
   To measure the extent and distribution of within-manuscript changes between preprint and published versions across multiple dimensions of statistical reporting and rigor.

2. **Associational analysis of contextual factors**  
   To examine how observed changes are statistically associated with funding structures, disciplinary norms, academic system context, and author career stage, without making causal claims about the effects of peer review.

Observed patterns are interpreted as **descriptive and associational**, not as evidence that peer review causes improvement, degradation, or convergence in research quality. The project is explicitly designed to accommodate heterogeneous outcomes, including minimal or null changes, which are treated as substantively informative given prevailing assumptions about the role of peer review.

The linkage between these conceptual aims and their operational measurement is made explicit in the following section, with full definitions and decision rules specified in the preregistered study protocol ([Peer-Review Impact Study — Planning & Preregistration](peer_review_planning_document.md)).

## From Motivation to Operationalization

Each conceptual motivation in this project is explicitly linked to preregistered, observable quantities to ensure that interpretation is grounded in measured evidence rather than post-hoc narrative. Full operational definitions, scoring rubrics, and analytic procedures are specified in the preregistered study protocol ([Peer-Review Impact Study — Planning & Preregistration](peer_review_planning_document.md)).

- **Manuscript change across fields and journals** is operationalized using within-manuscript change scores derived from preregistered indicators of statistical rigor and their composite summary measure (see [Preregistered Variables](peer_review_planning_document.md#7-preregistered-variables-v1v10) and [Scoring and Composite Measures](peer_review_planning_document.md#8-scoring-and-composite-measures)). Changes are summarized descriptively across disciplines, journal contexts, and preprint ecosystems.

- **Disciplinary review cultures** are operationalized through stratified comparisons of change magnitude and variability across broad disciplinary categories and time periods, as defined in the preregistered sampling frame and temporal scope (see [Sample Size Targets and Temporal Scope](peer_review_planning_document.md#93-sample-size-targets-and-temporal-scope) and [Sampling Frame and Corpus Definition](peer_review_planning_document.md#115-sampling-frame-and-corpus-definition)).

- **Funding environment and institutional incentives** are operationalized using contextual proxy variables (e.g., region and discipline-level funding norms) recorded as manuscript metadata and examined in association with preregistered change metrics (see [Analysis Plan](peer_review_planning_document.md#9-analysis-plan-highlevel) and  
  [Document Access and Eligibility](peer_review_planning_document.md#11-document-access-and-eligibility)).

- **Career stage effects** are operationalized using author-level metadata for first and corresponding authors, with revision patterns compared descriptively across career stages using preregistered change indicators (see [Preregistered Variables](peer_review_planning_document.md#7-preregistered-variables-v1v10) and [Appendix A: Preregistered Codebook](peer_review_planning_document.md#appendix-a-preregistered-codebook-for-statistical-rigor-indicators-v1v10)).

- **Empirical evaluation of whether observed changes are consistent with improved reporting** is operationalized through the direction, magnitude, and distribution of changes in reporting-focused indicators (e.g., uncertainty reporting, transparency, robustness), without treating improvement as a causal outcome (see [Hypotheses and Interpretation Framework](peer_review_planning_document.md#10-hypotheses-and-interpretation-framework) and [Transparency and Deviations](peer_review_planning_document.md#12-transparency-and-deviations)).

This structure ensures that each motivating question is evaluated using predefined, auditable measurements, while preserving a clear separation between conceptual interpretation and methodological specification.

## Impact

This project has both **practical** and **intellectual** implications across multiple stakeholders.

- **For authors**, the findings provide empirical context on the *types and magnitudes of changes* that commonly occur between preprint and published versions across disciplines and journal environments, helping to inform manuscript preparation, submission expectations, and revision strategy.

- **For trainees and early-career researchers**, the results offer data-driven insight into disciplinary review cultures, supporting more informed decisions about doctoral training, career paths, and research environments.

- **For policymakers, funders, and research administrators**, the study contributes systematic evidence to ongoing discussions about how peer review is associated with research reporting practices, institutional incentives, and academic autonomy, without assuming that observed changes are uniformly beneficial.

More broadly, this work aims to move beyond anecdote and selective case studies by establishing a **quantitative, transparent foundation** for evaluating how peer review operates across scientific contexts, thereby informing future methodological research and evidence-based discussions about potential reforms.

These implications are grounded in preregistered, manuscript-level measurements and are interpreted in accordance with the non-causal framework specified in the study protocol.
