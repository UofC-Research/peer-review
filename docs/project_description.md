# Publish or Perish: Quantifying the Impact of Peer Review on Scientific Work

## Background

In academic science, the mantra "publish or perish" underscores the centrality of publication to career survival across
disciplines. Peer review is the cornerstone of this process, but its conventions and outcomes vary widely. In some
disciplines, peer reviewers demand sweeping revisions; in others, only minor editorial changes are requested. While some
review suggestions may enhance scientific rigor, others may compromise authors' original vision, often due to pressure
to conform to journal norms.

This variability raises an important question: does peer review improve scientific quality, or does it hinder academic
freedom?

In fields such as biomedical research, extensive post-review modifications can misalign with the original scientific
intent. Authors' willingness to comply with reviewer comments may also vary depending on institutional pressure, funding
conditions, or career stages. Satirical cartoons like those from Jason Yao and CartoonStock's academic section reflect
the growing tension between authors and the peer review process.

## Aims

The goal of this project is to empirically quantify how much peer review changes academic papers, and to analyze what
institutional, disciplinary, and career-based factors correlate with those changes. Specifically, we aim to:

- Quantify textual and structural changes between preprint versions (from platforms like bioRxiv, arXiv, and medRxiv)
  and their corresponding published journal articles.
- Statistically associate the magnitude and type of change with:
  - Funding mechanisms: soft-funded (grant-dependent salaries) vs. hard-funded (institutionally supported).
  - Regional academic systems: differences across American, European, and Asian institutional contexts.
  - Career stages: from early-career researchers to tenured faculty.
  - Disciplines: including Math, Statistics, Computer Science, Physics, Chemistry, Biology, and Medicine.
- Evaluate the peer review process itself, identifying whether it more often serves to improve rigor or suppress
  original ideas.

## Impact

This project has practical and intellectual implications:

- For authors: it provides insight into what kinds of changes are typically expected in different fields and journals,
  helping to guide manuscript preparation and submission strategy.
- For students: it offers data-driven guidance for those considering PhDs, especially in evaluating the pressures and
  review cultures of different disciplines.
- For policymakers and funders: it contributes to the debate on how peer review affects research quality and academic
  freedom.

Ultimately, this work seeks to move beyond anecdotal complaints and provide a quantitative foundation for reforming or
rethinking the peer review process.
